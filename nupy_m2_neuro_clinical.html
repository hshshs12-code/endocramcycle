Clinical approach to a seizure

  1. HistoryCollect information from the patient and witnesses; use clinical reasoning to classify the seizure; consider reversible causes
  2. Physical examNeurological exam and injuries; fundoscopy (CNS lesion); stigmata of Sturge-Weber, tuberous sclerosis, Chédiak-Higashi
  3. InvestigationsBy etiology: CBC, electrolytes, glucose, toxicology, LP/CSF if infection suspected; EEG if epilepsy suspected (24-h EEG ↑ yield; normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy)
  4. ImagingMRI is the image of choice; CT if stroke or intracranial hemorrhage is suspected
  5. ManagementDepends on the type. During a tonic-clonic seizure: do not try to stop the movements, put nothing in the mouth; drugs per pharmacology

Status epilepticus — timeline

  1. Old definitionSingle seizure > 30 min, or a series without regaining function between seizures over 30 min
  2. Operational definition≥ 5 min of continuous seizures, or ≥ 2 discrete seizures with incomplete recovery of consciousness between them
  3. ≤ 5 minInitiate drug therapy within 5 minutes of seizure onset
  4. > 30 minBrain damage possible; poor outcome and risk of death

Classification (ILAE 2017)Onset: focal (one part of cortex, previously 'partial'), generalized (entire cortex simultaneously, loss of consciousness) or unknown. Focal → aware or impaired awareness; each onset → motor or non-motor. Focal motor: automatisms, atonic, clonic, epileptic spasms, hyperkinetic, myoclonic, tonic; focal non-motor: autonomic, behavior arrest, cognitive, emotional, sensory.
