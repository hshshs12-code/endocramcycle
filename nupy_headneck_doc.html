p.1Bones of the head & neck · sutures · wormian bones · pterionSkull & cranial fossae
Talbeh Head and Neck Anatomy.pdf [summary] · p.1 · tap to enlarge

Teaching script

The skull splits into two sets of bones. The viscerocranium is the face: zygomatic, maxilla, nasal, lacrimal and mandible in front, with the vomer, palatine and inferior conchae hidden deeper. The neurocranium is the box around the brain: frontal, parietal, temporal, sphenoid, occipital and, tucked into the floor in the midline, the ethmoid.

The bones meet at sutures. The sagittal suture runs between the two parietals, the coronal suture crosses in front of them, and the two meet at the bregma. The lambdoid suture separates the parietals from the occipital, and the squamous suture runs over the temporal bone. Sometimes extra ossification centres appear inside a suture and make small islands of bone: wormian bones. They love the lambdoid suture and the posterior fontanelle, and the exam point is that on an X-ray they can be mistaken for fractures.

The one suture landmark you must know is the pterion, where frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid meet on the side of the head. It is the thinnest part of the skull, and directly under it runs the middle meningeal artery. Hit the pterion and the artery tears, arterial blood peels the dura off the bone, and you get an epidural haematoma.

Viscerocranium

  • Anterior view of the face: zygomatic, maxilla, nasal, lacrimal, mandible (hand-labelled nasal, lacrimal, zygomatic, maxilla, mandible).
  • The vomer, palatine and inferior conchae bones lie deep within the face.

Neurocranium

  • Lateral view: frontal, parietal, temporal, sphenoid, occipital.
  • Superior internal view: frontal, ethmoid (cribriform plate in the midline), sphenoid, temporal, parietal, occipital.

Sutures of the skull

  • Sagittal suture between the parietal bones; coronal suture (frontal – parietal) meeting the sagittal at the bregma; lambdoid suture (parietal – occipital); squamous suture (temporal – parietal); sphenofrontal, sphenoparietal, sphenosquamous, parietomastoid and occipitomastoid sutures.

Wormian / intrasutural bones

  • Abnormal bones that develop from extra ossification centres within the cranium (extra bone pieces).
  • Most frequently in the lambdoid suture or the coronal suture, and seen in the fontanelles, particularly the posterior fontanelle.
  • Can be mis-identified as fractures on radiographs.

Pterion

  • H-shaped junction of frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid on the lateral skull; internal view shows the middle meningeal artery grooving the bone beneath it; coronal inset of an epidural haematoma with dura pushed away from the skull.

Teaching script

Before the sutures fuse, the infant skull has fontanelles: the anterior one between frontal and parietals, the posterior at the lambda, and the smaller sphenoidal and mastoid ones at the sides. If a suture fuses too early you get craniosynostosis, and the shape names the suture: sagittal fusion gives a long narrow scaphocephaly, both coronal sutures give a short brachycephaly, one coronal gives an oblique plagiocephaly, the metopic suture gives a triangular trigonocephaly, and everything at once gives a tower skull.

Then the three cranial fossae, which are really a list of holes. Anterior fossa: the cribriform plate for the olfactory nerve, and the foramen caecum for an emissary vein. Middle fossa, from front to back: the optic canal for the optic nerve and ophthalmic artery; the superior orbital fissure for three, four, V one and six plus the ophthalmic veins; foramen rotundum for V two; foramen ovale for V three, the lesser petrosal nerve, the accessory meningeal artery and an emissary vein; foramen spinosum for the middle meningeal artery; and foramen lacerum with the nerve of the pterygoid canal and the ascending pharyngeal artery. Posterior fossa: the internal acoustic meatus for seven, eight and the labyrinthine artery, the jugular foramen for nine, ten and eleven, the hypoglossal canal for twelve, and the foramen magnum for the spinal root of eleven, the spinal arteries and the meninges. An acoustic neuroma sits in that internal acoustic meatus and squeezes the eighth nerve.

Fontanelles

  • Lateral view: sphenoidal and mastoid fontanelles (asterion), coronal / squamous / lambdoid sutures. Superior view: anterior fontanelle (frontal – sagittal – coronal) and posterior fontanelle (sagittal – lambdoid).
  • Craniosynostosis — type depends on the suture that fuses early: coronal bilateral = brachycephaly (short head), coronal unilateral = anterior plagiocephaly (oblique head); sagittal = scaphocephaly (narrow and long head); metopic = trigonocephaly (triangular head); lambdoid = posterior plagiocephaly (flat head); multiple sutures = acrocephaly / oxycephaly / turricephaly (tower skull), Kleeblattschädel (cloverleaf skull).

Anterior cranial fossa

  • Frontal crest, crista galli, cribriform plate and its foramina, foramen caecum, orbital part of the frontal bone, lesser wing of sphenoid, anterior clinoid process, body of sphenoid.

Middle cranial fossa

  • Hypophysial fossa, tuberculum sellae, chiasmatic sulcus, middle / posterior clinoid processes, dorsum sellae, greater wing of sphenoid, trigeminal impression, arcuate eminence, tegmen tympani; foramina: optic canal, superior orbital fissure, foramen rotundum, foramen ovale, foramen spinosum, foramen lacerum, groove and hiatus for the greater and lesser petrosal nerves, opening of the carotid canal, groove for the middle meningeal artery.

Posterior cranial fossa

  • Clivus, jugular tubercle, internal acoustic meatus, jugular foramen, hypoglossal canal, foramen magnum, grooves for the inferior petrosal, sigmoid and transverse sinuses, internal occipital crest and protuberance.

Teaching script

The orbit is built from seven bones, and it has three doors at the back. The superior orbital fissure carries the three eye-movement nerves, three, four and six, plus the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal with its lacrimal, frontal and nasociliary branches, and the superior ophthalmic vein. The inferior orbital fissure carries the inferior ophthalmic vein, the zygomatic branch of the maxillary nerve and sympathetic fibres. The optic canal carries only the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery.

Fractures come in two flavours. A rim fracture breaks the maxilla, zygomatic and frontal bones at their sutures. A blow-out fracture is blunt force on the eyeball itself: the pressure escapes through the weakest walls, which are the medial wall into the ethmoid sinus and the floor into the maxillary sinus, and orbital contents herniate with it. Any orbital fracture raises orbital pressure and pushes the eye forward.

A fracture through the skull base announces itself with fluid and bruises: CSF from the ear or the nose, raccoon eyes around the orbits, and Battle sign over the mastoid. And the Le Fort levels of the face: one is a floating palate, two is a floating maxilla in a pyramid, three is the whole face separated from the neurocranium. They come from severe blunt trauma and rarely alone.

Orbit

  • Bones: frontal, ethmoid, lacrimal, sphenoid, palatine, zygomatic, maxilla.
  • Foramina and contents — superior orbital fissure: oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV), ophthalmic division of trigeminal (V1 — lacrimal, frontal, nasociliary), abducens (VI), superior ophthalmic vein. Inferior orbital fissure: inferior ophthalmic vein, zygomatic branch of the maxillary nerve, sympathetic nerves. Optic canal: optic nerve (II), ophthalmic artery.

Orbit fractures

  • 1 Orbital rim fracture — fracture of the bones forming the outer rim of the bony orbit (maxilla, zygomatic and frontal), usually at the sutures joining the three bones.
  • 2 'Blowout' fracture — partial herniation of orbital contents through one of its walls due to blunt-force trauma to the eye; medial and inferior walls weakest, with contents herniating into the ethmoid and maxillary sinuses respectively.
  • Any orbital fracture leads to increased orbital pressure — causing exophthalmos (protrusion of the eye).

Basilar skull fractures

  • Occur anywhere along the base of the skull, in at least one of the temporal, occipital, sphenoid, frontal or ethmoid bones. Signs: otorrhoea (fluid from the ear), rhinorrhoea (fluid from the nose), periorbital ecchymosis (raccoon eyes), postauricular ecchymosis (Battle sign). Ecchymosis = skin discoloration from blood entering the tissue; a bruise is always trauma, ecchymosis not always.

Le Fort fractures I, II, III

  • Le Fort I — floating palate (horizontal fracture separating palate from maxilla). Le Fort II — floating maxilla (pyramidal fracture separating the maxilla). Le Fort III — floating face (horizontal fracture separating viscerocranium from neurocranium).
  • Result of severe blunt-force trauma to the face; rarely in isolation, frequently with other skull fractures.

Teaching script

The scalp is five layers and spells its own name: skin, connective tissue, aponeurosis of the epicranius, loose connective tissue, pericranium. The loose layer is the danger area, because blood and infection spread through it easily and it connects forward into the eyelids and the root of the nose; that is why a scalp injury can present as raccoon eyes.

Three arteries feed most of the scalp, and all three come off the external carotid: superficial temporal, occipital and posterior auricular. On the face the equivalent structural layer is the SMAS, the superficial musculo-aponeurotic system, supplied by the transverse facial artery and lifted in a facelift.

Then the danger triangle of the face, from the bridge of the nose to the corners of the mouth. Its veins have no valves. A nasal tributary drains into the angular vein, the angular vein into the superior and inferior ophthalmic veins, and those empty into the cavernous sinus. A squeezed pimple in that triangle can therefore seed a cavernous sinus thrombosis.

Head & neck — layers of the scalp

  • SCALP: Skin, Connective tissue (dense), Aponeurotic layer (epicranius), Loose connective tissue, Pericranium.

Arterial supply of the scalp

  • Superficial temporal, occipital and posterior auricular arteries (maxillary artery labelled beneath).

SMAS — superficial musculo-aponeurotic system

  • Layers of the face: 1 skin, 2 subcutaneous, 3 musculo-aponeurotic, 4 retaining ligaments and space, 5 periosteum and deep fascia.

Danger area of the face

  • The triangle from the bridge of the nose to the corners of the mouth; lateral view of the facial veins — supratrochlear, supra-orbital, nasofrontal, angular vein, vorticose veins, superior and inferior ophthalmic veins, cavernous sinus, pterygoid plexus, maxillary, deep facial, retromandibular and facial veins.

Teaching script

Every muscle that moves the face is a facial nerve muscle. The orbital and nasal group, orbicularis oculi and nasalis and corrugator, the oral group around the mouth, and also the two the students forget: platysma in the neck and the auricular muscles around the ear. All of them are muscles of facial expression, so all of them are seventh nerve, branchial efferent axons.

The nerve itself leaves the skull through the stylomastoid foramen, gives off the posterior auricular branch, then enters the parotid and fans into five terminal branches: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular and cervical. To Zanzibar by motor car. Because the nerve has a long intracranial course before that foramen and an extracranial course after it, a Bell palsy can come from either side of the skull.

Sensation of the face is a different nerve entirely. The trigeminal divides into V one over the forehead and nose, V two over the cheek and upper lip, and V three over the lower lip, chin and jaw, each leaving through its own foramen. But look at the map: the angle of the jaw and the neck are cervical nerves from the superficial cervical plexus, not the trigeminal.

Muscles of the face

  • Orbital + nasal group: corrugator supercilii, orbicularis oculi (orbital and palpebral parts), nasalis, levator labii superioris alaeque nasi. Oral group: zygomaticus major and minor, levator labii superioris, levator anguli oris, buccinator, orbicularis oris, risorius, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris, mentalis, platysma. 'Appreciate the direction of the muscle fibres and consider the action of these muscles.'

Platysma & auricularis

  • Platysma over the neck (external jugular vein beneath); auricularis anterior, superior and posterior; frontal belly / occipital belly of epicranius, temporoparietalis.

Voluntary / somatic innervation of the face

  • Facial nerve leaves the stylomastoid foramen, gives the posterior auricular branch, then inside the parotid the five terminal branches: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular, cervical — To Zanzibar By Motor Car.

Cutaneous innervation of the face

  • Trigeminal divisions in relation to the skull and foramina: V1 ophthalmic (superior orbital fissure; supraorbital nerves, ciliary ganglion), V2 maxillary (foramen rotundum, pterygopalatine ganglion, infraorbital nerve), V3 mandibular (foramen ovale, otic ganglion, lingual, submandibular ganglion, mental nerve).
  • Dermatome map: V1 forehead / upper lid / nose, V2 cheek / upper lip, V3 lower lip / chin / jaw / temple; cervical nerves (superficial cervical plexus) over the angle of the jaw, neck and back of the head.

Teaching script

Two arteries on the face come from inside the skull: the supraorbital and supratrochlear are branches of the ophthalmic, which is the internal carotid. Everything else is external carotid: the facial artery winding up to end as the angular artery, the transverse facial and the frontal and parietal branches of the superficial temporal, and the mental branch from the inferior alveolar. Veins follow the arteries and share their names. You can feel three of these: the temporal pulse in front of the ear, the carotid pulse in the neck, and the facial pulse at the edge of the mandible.

Lymph from the face collects in the submental, submandibular, parotid, mastoid and occipital nodes, but all of it ends up in the deep cervical nodes, then the jugular lymphatic trunk, then the thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct.

Vasculature of the face

  • From the ophthalmic artery (internal carotid): supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries. From the external carotid: the facial artery (terminates as the angular artery; labial vessels), the transverse facial artery and frontal / parietal branches of the superficial temporal artery, the mental artery (inferior alveolar, from the maxillary). Veins usually follow the arteries and share names, but are variable; posterior auricular, occipital, external and internal jugular veins.

Pulse points

  • Temporal pulse (superficial temporal artery, anterior branch), carotid pulse (common carotid artery), facial pulse (facial artery at the mandible).

Lymphatic drainage of the face

  • Occipital, mastoid, pre-auricular and parotid, submandibular and submental nodes → upper deep cervical nodes.

Teaching script

The parotid region is a box with the gland inside it. Its borders are the masseter in front, the ear and sternocleidomastoid behind, the zygomatic arch above, the angle of the mandible below, and the ramus of the mandible on the inside. Inside the box: the parotid gland and its duct, the parotid plexus of the facial nerve with its five branches, the retromandibular vein, the external carotid artery and the masseter. One detail from the notes: the posterior auricular branch of the facial nerve is given off before the nerve enters the gland, so it does not pierce the parotid.

Parotid region — boundaries and contents

  • Boundaries: anteriorly the anterior border of masseter; posteriorly the external ear and anterior border of sternocleidomastoid; superiorly the zygomatic arch; inferiorly the angle and inferior border of the mandible; medially the ramus of the mandible (behind masseter).
  • Contents: parotid gland and parotid duct, parotid plexus of the facial nerve (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical branches), retromandibular vein, external carotid artery, masseter muscle.

Teaching script

The parotid is wedged between the ramus of the mandible and the mastoid process, and three things pass through it in layers. The facial nerve is most superficial; it splits the gland into superficial and deep parts and forms the parotid plexus. Deeper is the retromandibular vein, formed by the superficial temporal and maxillary veins. Deepest is the external carotid artery, which gives the posterior auricular artery and then ends as the maxillary and superficial temporal arteries. The duct leaves the front of the gland, runs over the masseter, pierces the buccinator and opens opposite the second upper molar.

The gland's own innervation is the exam favourite. Sensation is the auriculotemporal nerve from V three. Parasympathetic secretomotor fibres start in the glossopharyngeal nerve, travel as the lesser petrosal nerve, synapse in the otic ganglion, and then hitch a ride on that same auriculotemporal nerve to reach the gland. Sympathetics come from T one through the superior cervical ganglion.

Frey syndrome follows parotid surgery: the cut parasympathetic fibres regrow into the sympathetic pathway to the sweat glands of the cheek, so the patient sweats and flushes over the parotid whenever they eat.

Parotid gland

  • Irregular shape, wedged between the mandibular ramus and mastoid process; relations: medial pterygoid, mandible, masseter, styloid process, retromandibular vein, external carotid artery, facial nerve.
  • Structures passing through the gland: 1 facial nerve (VII) — separates the gland into superficial and deep parts and forms the parotid plexus (five terminal branches); 2 retromandibular vein — union of superficial temporal and maxillary veins, superficial to the external carotid and deep to the facial nerve; 3 external carotid artery — gives the posterior auricular artery then divides into maxillary and superficial temporal arteries.
  • The parotid duct runs from the anterior surface of the gland, passes over the masseter, pierces the buccinator and enters the mouth lateral to the 2nd maxillary molar (buccal branch of facial n., buccal fat pad, SMAS on the figure).

Innervation of the parotid

  • Sensory: auriculotemporal nerve (V3). Parasympathetic (GVE): preganglionic fibres in the lesser petrosal nerve of CN IX (tympanic nerve / plexus) → synapse in the otic ganglion → postganglionic fibres ride the auriculotemporal nerve. Sympathetic (GVE): from T1 via the superior cervical ganglion along the external carotid.

Frey's syndrome

  • Photos before and while eating: sweaty, reddish skin over the parotid region beside the surgical scar. Proposed mechanism: after injury the parasympathetic secretomotor fibres regenerate into the sympathetic pathway to the sweat glands (mixed fibres).

Dura, arachnoid, pia, from superficial to deep. Cranially the dura is two layers, periosteal and meningeal, stuck to the inside of the skull, so the epidural space is only potential and only opens when an artery bleeds into it. In the spine the dura is a single meningeal layer hanging in the canal and ending at S two, so the spinal epidural space is real, filled with fat and veins, and that is where epidural anaesthetic goes. Arachnoid clings to the dura in both. Pia clings to the brain and cord, and in the spine it also reaches out to the dura as the denticulate ligaments.

Between arachnoid and pia is the subarachnoid space, which widens into cisterns full of CSF: chiasmatic, interpeduncular, pontine, cisterna magna. Arachnoid villi push up through the dura into the venous sinuses and return CSF to the blood.

The dural venous sinuses live in the pockets between the two dural layers, collect the brain's venous blood and its CSF, and drain to the internal jugular vein. The four major ones are the superior sagittal, straight, transverse and sigmoid sinuses; the minor ones are the inferior sagittal, occipital and the superior and inferior petrosal; and then the cavernous sinuses, joined across the midline by the intercavernous sinuses.

Layers of the meninges

  • Scalp, skull, periosteal dura, meningeal dura, arachnoid, subarachnoid space, pia, cerebral cortex; arachnoid villus into the superior sagittal sinus, subdural space, falx cerebri (in the longitudinal fissure only).

Cranial vs spinal

  • Dura: cranial double-layered (periosteal + meningeal) attached to the inner skull; spinal single meningeal layer suspended in the vertebral canal, ends at S2. Epidural space: cranial potential space between dura and skull; spinal real space between dura and vertebral periosteum. Arachnoid: attached to the inner surface of the dura in both. Pia: attached to the CNS surface; spinally also to the dura as denticulate ligaments.

Cisterns

  • Chiasmatic, interpeduncular, pontine cisterns, spinal subarachnoid space, cisterna magna (figures A–D, Nolte).

The nose has two autonomic supplies that share one road. Parasympathetic: facial nerve, greater petrosal nerve, nerve of the pterygoid canal, synapse in the pterygopalatine ganglion, nasal branches; this one makes mucus. Sympathetic: superior cervical ganglion, internal carotid plexus, deep petrosal nerve, the same nerve of the pterygoid canal, straight through the ganglion without synapsing, nasal branches; this one constricts vessels and dries the mucosa.

Clinical bits: a deviated septum blocks airflow and is fixed by septoplasty; the nasal bone is the most commonly fractured facial bone; and a bad nasal fracture can crack the cribriform plate, letting CSF drip from the nose and giving bacteria a path to the meninges. Sinusitis blocks the drainage channels and hurts over the face, rhinitis is inflamed nasal mucosa, allergic or not, and polyps are the usual obstruction.

Two dental blocks finish the page. The inferior alveolar block goes in at the mandibular foramen and numbs the lower teeth, lower lip and chin; if the needle strays it reaches the parotid and the facial nerve, giving a temporary facial palsy, or the medial pterygoid. The full mandibular block goes through the mandibular notch into the infratemporal fossa and takes the auriculotemporal, buccal, lingual and inferior alveolar nerves together.

Nasal cavity innervation — parasympathetic path

  • Facial nerve (VII) → greater petrosal nerve → nerve of the pterygoid canal → pterygopalatine ganglion (synapse) → nasal branches; controls mucus secretion keeping the mucosa moist.

Nasal cavity innervation — sympathetic path

  • Superior cervical ganglion → internal carotid plexus → deep petrosal nerve → nerve of the pterygoid canal → fibres pass through the pterygopalatine ganglion without synapsing → nasal branches; regulates blood flow and glandular secretion (vasoconstriction, decreased secretion).

Clinical correlates

Nasal fractures — most common facial fracture; nasal deformation. CSF rhinorrhoea — a severe nasal fracture may fracture the cribriform plate of the ethmoid, breaching the meninges so CSF drains from the subarachnoid space into the nose; may lead to meningitis.
  • Sinusitis — inflammation of the paranasal sinus mucosa obstructing the connecting channels (tenderness and fullness over the face); pansinusitis if several; viral infection, nasal polyps or a deviated septum. Rhinitis — allergic (type 1 hypersensitivity, 'hay fever') or non-allergic (infection, irritants, temperature change) or obstruction, most commonly nasal polyps (swollen mucosa with neutrophils); both together = rhinosinusitis.

Lymph from the front of the tongue goes to the submental and submandibular nodes; from the back, base and tonsils it goes straight to the deep cervical nodes; and everything ends in the jugular trunks and the thoracic or right lymphatic duct. Some of it crosses to the other side, especially from the base, which is why tongue cancers can spread bilaterally.

Testing the hypoglossal nerve is asking the patient to stick out the tongue. The genioglossus on the weak side cannot push, so the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion.

The submandibular and sublingual glands are supplied through the chorda tympani: parasympathetic fibres leave the facial nerve, cross the middle ear, exit through the petrotympanic fissure, join the lingual nerve in the infratemporal fossa, synapse in the submandibular ganglion and reach the glands. The same chorda tympani carries the taste fibres from the front of the tongue back the other way. Sympathetics arrive from the superior cervical ganglion along the facial artery and cut secretion down. A salivary stone, a sialolith, forms most often in the submandibular duct and gives pain and swelling that flares whenever saliva is stimulated.

Lymphatic drainage of the tongue

  • Submental and submandibular nodes drain the anterior tongue; deep cervical nodes drain the posterior tongue, base and tonsils (via the jugulofacial venous junction / jugular nodes).   • Checks CN XII: weakness gives ipsilateral weakness of protrusion, so the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion (paralysed genioglossus on the affected side).

The maxillary artery is the bigger terminal branch of the external carotid and it has three parts, each named by where it is. Part one, the mandibular part, runs behind the mandible and gives two big branches, the middle meningeal artery up through foramen spinosum and the inferior alveolar artery down into the mandibular canal, plus the small deep auricular, anterior tympanic and accessory meningeal. The nasopharynx is where the pharyngotympanic tube opens, behind the torus tubarius, with the pharyngeal tonsil in its roof and the salpingopharyngeal fold running down from the tube. The tube equalises middle-ear pressure and is pulled open by three muscles: salpingopharyngeus, tensor veli palatini and levator veli palatini. The oropharynx is framed by two arches, palatoglossal in front and palatopharyngeal behind, with the palatine tonsil between them, the uvula above and the lingual tonsil and valleculae below. All the tonsils together form Waldeyer's ring: pharyngeal, tubal, palatine and lingual, guarding the entrances for air and food.

Tonsillectomy is done for obstructive hypertrophy, for recurrent tonsillitis by the seven-five-three rule, and for suspected malignancy. Two things are at risk: the tonsillar branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve running in the lateral wall, which is why patients get ear pain afterwards, and the internal carotid artery just posterolateral to the tonsil. The tonsil's blood supply is mainly the facial artery.

The one to know cold is the posterior cricoarytenoid, the only abductor, the only muscle that opens the airway. The transverse and oblique arytenoids and the lateral cricoarytenoid adduct. The cricothyroid tilts the thyroid cartilage forward and tenses the folds, and the vocalis fine-tunes them. The mnemonic is pat the cat very lightly.

Innervation: everything is recurrent laryngeal nerve except the cricothyroid, which is the external laryngeal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Sensation above the folds is the internal laryngeal nerve, coming through the thyrohyoid membrane; sensation below the folds, including the folds, is the recurrent laryngeal nerve, which climbs in the groove between trachea and oesophagus, enters under the inferior constrictor and becomes the inferior laryngeal nerve.

The true vocal folds are the vocal ligament plus the vocalis muscle, and they vibrate over a hundred times a second. The vestibular folds above them are false and silent. The gap between the true folds is the rima glottidis; between the false folds, the rima vestibuli. Voice needs air, vibration and resonance; if the cords are stuck near the midline you get stridor.

Intrinsic muscles of the larynx

  • Functions: adjust vocal-fold tension, open and close the rima glottidis, control the vestibule, close the laryngeal inlet. The larynx is highly mobile and is moved up / down and forward / back by extrinsic muscles attached to it or the hyoid.
  • Table (all CN X, recurrent laryngeal unless stated): posterior cricoarytenoid — abduction and external rotation of the arytenoid; the primary abductor and opener of the rima glottidis. Arytenoid oblique — sphincter of the laryngeal inlet. Transverse arytenoid — adduction of the arytenoids. Thyroarytenoid — sphincter of the vestibule and inlet. Cricothyroid — CN X external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve: forward-downward rotation of the thyroid cartilage at the cricothyroid joint (tenses). Aryepiglottic — adducts, sphincter on the inlet. Thyroepiglottic — depresses the epiglottis. Vocalis — adjusts tension in the vocal folds. Lateral cricoarytenoid — internal rotation of the arytenoid and adduction of the folds. Mnemonic 'Pat the cat very lightly: PATTh(e)CATVL'.

True vocal folds

  • True vocal cords = true vocal folds + vocalis muscle. Vocal ligament (just inferior to the cords) vibrates 130–180 times per second (men / women); vocalis originates from the lateral arytenoid and inserts into the ipsilateral vocal ligament. False vocal cords (vestibular folds) sit above and do not produce sound. Voice needs a generator (air from the lungs), vibration (folds) and resonance (cavities); normal voice needs the folds to meet in the midline. Dysphonia = voice disorder; stridor = high-pitched musical sound from obstructed airflow with the cords stuck para-median. Superior view: epiglottis, vocal fold, vestibular fold, aryepiglottic fold, cuneiform and corniculate tubercles, interarytenoid fold; rima vestibuli = space between vestibular folds, rima glottidis = space between vocal folds.

Arterial supply & innervation of the larynx

  • Vessels: superior thyroid → superior laryngeal artery, cricothyroid branch; inferior thyroid (thyrocervical trunk) → inferior laryngeal artery. Superior laryngeal nerve (X) divides at the hyoid into the internal laryngeal (through the thyrohyoid membrane; GSA to the laryngeal cavity above the vocal folds) and the external laryngeal (motor to cricothyroid and inferior constrictor). Recurrent laryngeal nerve (X): GSA below the folds (including the folds) and BE motor to all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid; ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove, enters deep to the inferior constrictor (gap 4) and becomes the inferior laryngeal nerve after the cricothyroid joint.
