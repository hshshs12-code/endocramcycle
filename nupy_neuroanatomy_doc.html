Teaching script

Page one sets up the coordinates for the whole course. Three planes of section: coronal cuts the brain front to back like slices of a loaf, sagittal splits it left from right, and horizontal is a view from above. The word to remember is somatotopy: the body is mapped onto the cortex in an orderly way, and the map is warped, because the hands and the face get far more cortex than the trunk. That warped map is the homunculus, and notice that the leg and foot hang over the medial surface in the paracentral lobule.

The rest of the page is the Brodmann map, and this is pure memorisation. Area four is primary motor, area six is premotor and supplementary motor, area eight is the frontal eye field. Forty-four and forty-five are Broca. Three, one, two is primary somatosensory, five and seven are its association areas. Forty-one and forty-two are primary auditory, twenty-two is Wernicke. Seventeen is primary visual, eighteen and nineteen are visual association. The exam trick is simple: give you a number and ask for the lobe or the function, or give you a deficit and ask for the number.

Orientation terms — planes of section

  • Coronal (frontal), mid-sagittal (medial) and horizontal sections; axes superior–dorsal / inferior–ventral, anterior / posterior (hand-labelled R / L, front / back on each cut).

Somatotopy

  • The orderly representation of body parts in cortex.
  • More neurons are allocated to body regions used more frequently — hands and face have a greater representation in both primary motor and primary somatosensory cortex.
  • Homunculus map of the motor (precentral) and somatosensory (postcentral) cortices; the leg / foot / genitalia sit in the paracentral lobule on the medial surface.

Brodmann regions

  • Histological map of the cortex by cytoarchitecture (numbers).
  • Primary cortex directly receives sensory information or directly instructs lower motor neurons: primary motor (4), primary somatosensory (3,1,2), primary auditory (41, 42), primary visual (17).

Cortical areas by lobe

  • Parietal: primary somatosensory 3,1,2; somatosensory association 5 and 7; sensory speech (Wernicke): superior temporal gyrus 22, supramarginal gyrus 40, angular gyrus 39.
  • Frontal: primary motor 4; motor association 6 (premotor 6, supplementary motor 6); frontal eye field 8; motor speech (Broca) 44, 45; prefrontal 9, 10, 11, 12.
  • Temporal: primary auditory 41, 42; auditory association 22.
  • Occipital: primary visual 17; visual association 18, 19.

Teaching script

Grey matter is cell bodies and dendrites, white matter is myelinated axons. The high-yield twist is that the arrangement flips between cord and brain: in the spinal cord the grey is inside, shaped like a butterfly, with white matter around it; in the brain the grey is on the outside as cortex and the white matter is inside. Stains invert the colours, so do not be fooled by a stained section.

Four lobes take their names from the skull bones over them, and three sulci divide them: the central sulcus between frontal and parietal, the lateral sulcus between temporal and the two lobes above it, and the parieto-occipital sulcus at the back. Pry open the lateral sulcus and you find the fifth lobe, the insula. Its anterior part handles taste, smell, visceral sensation and emotion; its posterior part handles hearing, touch and motor.

Finally, not all cortex is the same. Neocortex has six layers and makes up ninety-five percent. Allocortex has three layers, and there are exactly two examples: the hippocampus, which is archicortex, and the olfactory cortex, which is paleocortex. In between sits the transitional juxtallocortex, with four or five layers, in places like the cingulate and entorhinal cortex. The amygdala and basal forebrain have no layers at all.

White matter vs gray matter

  • Gray matter = neuronal cell bodies, dendrites and interneurons; white matter = myelinated axons.
  • In stained sections cell bodies appear white and axons dark; in wet sections cell bodies dark and axons white.
  • Spinal cord: gray internal, white external. Brain: gray external, white internal.

Lobes and major sulci

  • Four major lobes named for the skull bones over them: frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital.
  • Central sulcus separates frontal from parietal; lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure) separates temporal from frontal and parietal; parieto-occipital sulcus separates parietal from occipital.

The 5th lobe — the insula

  • Pry open the lateral sulcus to see the gyri of the insula.
  • Anterior insular cortex: olfactory, viscero-autonomic, gustatory and limbic (emotional) functions.
  • Posterior insular cortex: auditory, somatosensory and skeletomotor.

Cortex — four kinds

  • 1) Neocortex 'new cortex': 6-layered, 95 % of human cerebral cortex.
  • 2) Allocortex 'old cortex': 3-layered — archicortex (hippocampus), paleocortex (olfactory).
  • 3) Juxtallocortex / periallocortex (4–5 layers): entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex.
  • 4) Subcortical — no layers: amygdala, basal forebrain.
  • Figure: neocortex over mesocortex / allocortex over basal mid- and forebrain (hypothalamus, thalamus) over pontine tegmentum, medulla, peripheral & enteric nervous systems.

Teaching script

The six layers of neocortex have a division of labour. Layers one to three talk only to other cortex. Layer four is the input layer from the thalamus. Layer five is the big output layer to the striatum, brainstem and spinal cord, and it holds the giant Betz cells in the motor cortex. Layer six projects back to the thalamus. So the mnemonic is: four in, five out to the cord, six out to the thalamus.

That explains granular versus agranular cortex. Sensory areas receive a lot from the thalamus, so their layer four is thick: granular. Motor cortex sends rather than receives, so layer four is thin: agranular. Pyramidal cells are the only output neurons and they use glutamate; the non-pyramidal interneurons use GABA.

Cortex is also organised in vertical columns: every cell in a column does the same job, so an electrode pushed straight down stays in one function, while an oblique track crosses several.

Fibres come in three kinds. Association fibres stay in one hemisphere: the short U-fibres, the superior longitudinal and arcuate fasciculus, and the uncinate. Commissural fibres cross to the other hemisphere: the corpus callosum, the anterior commissure for the olfactory and temporal areas, and the posterior commissure, which links the pretectal nuclei and is the reason the pupillary light reflex is consensual.

The six layers have different inputs & outputs

  • Layer I is most superficial, VI deep.
  • Layers I–III receive inputs from cortex and output to other areas of cortex (association or commissural fibres) — do not project outside cortex.
  • Layer IV receives inputs from the thalamus.
  • Layer V outputs to the striatum, brainstem and spinal cord.
  • Layer VI outputs to the thalamus.
  • Pyramidal neurons (primarily II–III, V, VI): spiny dendrites, long axons to cortical or subcortical targets, glutamatergic.
  • Non-pyramidal (interneurons): most cortical interneurons are GABAergic.

Neocortex can be granular or agranular

  • Granular = well-developed layer 4 (primary sensory regions); agranular = smaller layer 4 (primary motor area 4, premotor area 6).
  • Green = agranular, blue = granular on the medial / lateral maps.

Cortical columns (visual-system example)

  • Orientation columns: a column in primary visual cortex responds to a bar of light in one orientation; a vertical electrode stays in one column, an oblique penetration crosses several.

Association fibres

  • Axons of pyramidal neurons from layers I, II, III; connect different cortical areas in one hemisphere: arcuate fibres (loop between adjacent gyri), superior longitudinal fasciculus, arcuate fasciculus, uncinate fasciculus; inferior occipito-frontal fasciculus on the figure.

Commissural fibres

  • Interconnect homologous (same) areas of the two hemispheres; axons from layers I, II, III.
  • 1) Corpus callosum: genu → anterior frontal lobes; body → posterior frontal, all parietal, superior temporal; splenium → occipital lobes (forceps minor / major).
  • 2) Anterior commissure: olfactory nuclei, amygdalas, anterior temporal lobes.
  • 3) Posterior commissure: connects the pretectal nuclei — pupillary light reflex.

Teaching script

The third fibre type is projection fibres, and they gather into the internal capsule. Learn it by limb. Anterior limb: thalamocortical and frontopontine fibres. Genu: corticobulbar fibres, the ones going to the cranial nerve nuclei. Posterior limb: the corticospinal tract, plus the sensory fibres coming back up, and the optic and auditory radiations at its tail. So a lacunar stroke in the posterior limb gives a dense pure motor hemiparesis.

The second half of the page is how the cortex is built. New neurons are born at the ventricular surface and climb radial glia like a railway to reach the cortical plate, and later-born neurons pass the earlier ones, so the cortex forms inside out. Three genes map onto three failures of that journey. Filamin: the neuron never boards the train, so it stays by the ventricle. That is periventricular heterotopia, X-linked dominant, and it presents as epilepsy in the second decade. Doublecortin: the neuron cannot stay on the train. In males that is lissencephaly, a smooth brain; in females it is subcortical band heterotopia, the double cortex. Reelin: the neuron cannot get off at the right stop, so the layers come out inverted. That is the Norman-Roberts form of lissencephaly.

Projection fibres / internal capsule

  • Connect the cortex to subcortical, brainstem and spinal-cord targets; form the internal capsule.
  • 1) Anterior limb: thalamocortical fibres, cortico-striatal fibres, fibres connecting putamen / globus pallidus with the caudate, cortex and thalamus → pons (frontopontine).
  • 2) Genu: mostly corticobulbar, some thalamocortical.
  • 3) Posterior limb: mainly corticospinal (anterior ⅔), some medial lemniscus and spinothalamic fibres, optic and acoustic radiations and a few corticobulbar fibres (corticofugal).
  • Figure labels: anterior thalamic radiation, frontopontine, corticopontine, superior thalamic radiation (somatosensory), auditory radiation (sublenticular), optic radiation (retrolenticular).

Overview: cortical layers & connections

  • Supragranular (I–III) intracortical / association / commissural; granular IV corticopetal from thalamus; infragranular V → striatum, brainstem, spinal cord; VI → thalamus.

Radial glia as the 'railway' for migrating neurons

  • Excitatory cortical neurons are generated by asymmetric division of progenitors lining the ventricular wall; new-born neurons leave the ventricular zone → intermediate zone (future white matter) → subplate → cortical plate → stop in the marginal zone at the pial border.
  • They climb strands of radial glia that span ependyma to pia like spokes; radial glia are themselves neuronal progenitors.

Taking the train — Filamin

  • Actin-binding protein for initiation of migration; mutation → periventricular heterotopia (FLNA, X-linked dominant — males die in utero; females have neurons collected near the ventricles, intelligence normal or mildly compromised, late-onset epilepsy in the second decade).

Staying on board — Doublecortin

  • Microtubule-associated protein for proper migration (LISX1); males: X-linked lissencephaly ('smooth brain', no gyri) or pachygyria with severe retardation; heterozygote females: double cortex (subcortical laminar band heterotopia, SBH).

Getting off the train — Reelin

  • Extracellular-matrix protein for cell–cell interaction and termination of migration, secreted by Cajal-Retzius cells under the pia; chromosome 7, LIS2; Norman-Roberts syndrome: inverted cortical lamination (younger neurons deeper), underdeveloped cerebellum, craniofacial abnormalities.

Teaching script

Three short ideas before the ventricles. Neural pruning: the cortex makes roughly twice as many neurons as it keeps, and they compete for trophic factors; the useful synapses survive. Neuroplasticity: the brain rewires with experience, so a blindfolded person's visual cortex starts processing sound and touch within days. And adult neurogenesis does happen, but in exactly two places: the hippocampus and the olfactory bulb. That pair is the exam fact.

Neural pruning

  • The cortex overproduces neurons (about twice as many as needed); they compete for neurotrophic factors and those that acquire enough survive — selection of the 'fittest', most useful synapses.

Neuroplasticity

  • The brain changes with the environment over time (Marian Diamond: newness, challenge, exercise, diet and love).
  • Blindfold someone for 2 days and the visual cortex re-organises to process sound and touch; a removed limb bud → 50 % of motor neurons survive vs 25 %.

Adult neurogenesis

  • Yes — but primarily in two areas: hippocampus & olfactory bulb.

Teaching script

The ventricular system is four chambers. Two lateral ventricles, each with a frontal horn, a body, an atrium, an occipital horn and a temporal horn. A midline third ventricle, pierced by the interthalamic adhesion. And the fourth ventricle between pons and cerebellum, with lateral recesses. Choroid plexus sits in the body and temporal horn of the lateral ventricles and in the roof of the third and fourth; it is where CSF is made. Keep the temporal horn in mind: its floor is the hippocampus, which is why it is the horn that changes in Alzheimer disease.

Ventricular system

  • Lateral ventricles, 3rd ventricle, 4th ventricle (3-D casts).
  • Parts: frontal horn, central part (body), atrium, occipital horn, temporal horn; interventricular (I-V) foramen; interthalamic adhesion through the 3rd; aqueduct; 4th ventricle with lateral recess.
  • Choroid plexus of the 3rd and 4th ventricles and of the lateral ventricles (circled on the gross specimen).

Teaching script

This page is the plumbing of cerebrospinal fluid. The connections first: the interventricular foramina of Monro join each lateral ventricle to the third; the cerebral aqueduct joins third to fourth; and from the fourth, CSF leaves through the single median aperture of Magendie into the cisterna magna and the two lateral apertures of Luschka. Medial Magendie, lateral Luschka.

The numbers: about one hundred and fifty millilitres present at any time, but four to five hundred made per day, so the whole volume turns over three or four times. Twenty percent is inside the ventricles, eighty percent is outside in the subarachnoid space and cisterns. The functions are buoyancy, cushioning and chemical housekeeping.

The circuit ends at the arachnoid villi, which drain CSF into the superior sagittal sinus. Block that reabsorption, for example by scarring after meningitis or subarachnoid haemorrhage, or overproduce it with a choroid plexus papilloma, and you get communicating hydrocephalus: every ventricle enlarges, pressure rises, the discs swell.

And the lumbar puncture: skin, subcutaneous fat, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura, arachnoid, and then CSF. Two pops: the flavum and the dura. Level L3-L4 or L4-L5 in adults, L4-L5 in a child.

Ventricular system: foramina

  • Interventricular foramina (Monro), left & right: lateral → third.
  • Cerebral aqueduct (Sylvius): third → fourth.
  • Median aperture (Magendie): fourth → cerebellomedullary cistern (cisterna magna).
  • Lateral apertures (Luschka), left & right: fourth → quadrigeminal cistern (superior cistern / cistern of the great cerebral vein).

Cerebrospinal fluid

  • Made by the choroid plexus; clear, colourless, odourless.
  • 150 ml adult; 400–500 ml produced and reabsorbed daily.
  • Functions: 1 mechanical support (brain 1500 g → ~25 g in CSF, less 'traction' on nerves and vessels); 2 protection — shock absorber, immune cells; 3 metabolic — ionic composition, nutrients, removes metabolites.
  • Two parts: internal (lateral ventricles, foramina, 3rd, aqueduct, 4th) = 20 %; external (subarachnoid spaces and cisterns) = 80 %; they communicate via the 4th-ventricle apertures.

Lumbar puncture / spinal tap

  • Needle pierces skin → subcutaneous tissue → supraspinous ligament → interspinous ligament → ligamentum flavum → epidural space (internal vertebral venous plexus) → dura → arachnoid → subarachnoid space; CSF drawn from the lumbar cistern.
  • Adult L3/L4 or L4/L5; child L4/L5; lying or sitting position.

CSF circulation (8 steps)

  • Secreted by the plexus of each lateral ventricle → interventricular foramina → 3rd (plexus adds more) → aqueduct → 4th (adds more) → two lateral + one median aperture → fills the subarachnoid space bathing brain and cord → reabsorbed at arachnoid villi into the dural venous sinuses.
  • Choroid plexus → ventricles → subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi → sinuses → internal jugular vein.

Communicating hydrocephalus

  • Causes: decreased CSF reabsorption (scarring from meningitis or subarachnoid haemorrhage); increased production (rare — choroid-plexus papilloma, children with a mass in the ventricles); normal-pressure hydrocephalus.
  • ↑ CSF → ↑ intracranial pressure; headache; papilloedema; CT hallmark = dilation of ALL ventricles; can cause herniation.
  • Key scenario: prior meningitis or SAH + headache + papilloedema on eye exam + enlarged ventricles on CT.

Teaching script

Hydrocephalus is a where-is-the-block question. Start with normal pressure hydrocephalus: communicating, opening pressure normal, ventricles large, and the stretched corona radiata fibres give the triad wet, wobbly and wacky: incontinence, a magnetic gait and dementia. It is one of the reversible dementias; a shunt fixes it.

Now the obstructive causes, and the rule is that ventricles upstream of the block dilate. Aqueductal stenosis: lateral and third ventricles enlarge, the fourth stays normal; think congenital X-linked or intrauterine infection, and an infant with a growing head. A colloid cyst sits in the roof of the third ventricle right at the foramen of Monro, so only the lateral ventricles swell, in a young adult with positional headaches. Chiari two pushes vermis, tonsils and medulla through the foramen magnum, squashing the fourth ventricle, and travels with spina bifida. Dandy-Walker is a missing vermis and a fourth ventricle blown up into a cyst.

Do not confuse any of these with hydrocephalus ex vacuo, where the ventricles simply grow to fill the space left by an atrophying brain; the sulci widen in proportion, and nothing is under pressure.

Normal pressure hydrocephalus

  • Enlarged ventricles on imaging with normal opening pressure on LP; compression of the corona radiata, stretching of descending cortical fibres; suspected mechanism impaired CSF absorption.
  • Dementia; urinary incontinence; abnormal gait — wide-based, gait apraxia (difficulty initiating walking), magnetic gait (feet stuck to floor). Classic triad wet, wobbly and wacky.
  • Resolves with removal of CSF — lumbar puncture, ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt draining CSF to the abdomen.

Aqueductal stenosis

  • Stenosis of the cerebral aqueduct blocks 3rd → 4th: lateral and third dilated, fourth normal or small.
  • Congenital narrowing (X-linked, boys) or intrauterine infection (rubella, CMV, toxoplasmosis, syphilis); presents with enlarging head circumference.

Colloid cyst

  • Benign slow-growing cyst at the roof of the third ventricle blocking the foramen of Monro; unilateral block → one lateral ventricle enlarges, bilateral → both; third and fourth remain normal; presents in young adults (20s–50s).

Chiari II malformation

  • Downward displacement of the cerebellum — vermis (midline), tonsils (small rounded lobes on the inferior surface), sometimes brainstem (medulla) — through the foramen magnum; brainstem compression may occur.

Bleeds are defined by the space they open up. The epidural space is only potential; it appears when the middle meningeal artery is torn by a skull fracture and arterial blood peels dura off bone, fast, into a lens shape that stops at the sutures. The subdural space appears when a bridging vein tears, so venous blood spreads slowly along the surface in a crescent, crossing sutures but stopping at the falx and tentorium. The subarachnoid space is real and full of CSF, so a ruptured aneurysm bleeds straight into it.

The table on the page maps source to bleed: meningeal artery to epidural, dural sinus to subdural or epidural, bridging vein to subdural, cerebral vessel to subarachnoid or into the brain itself.

Herniations follow from the pressure. Above the tentorium: central, uncal, subfalcine and transcalvarial. Below: upward cerebellar and tonsillar, and tonsillar is the fatal one because it squeezes the medulla. A Chiari malformation is the congenital version of tonsils in the foramen magnum.

Spaces in the cranial meninges

  • Epidural = potential space between dura and skull; subdural = potential space in the innermost dural layer near the dura–arachnoid interface; subarachnoid = normally present, CSF-filled, enlarged in cisterns. Bleeding can open up potential meningeal spaces.

Epidural vs subdural haematoma

  • Epidural: rapidly expanding arterial blood from a torn middle meningeal artery after skull fracture; dura pushed away from the skull.
  • Subdural: slowly expanding venous blood from a torn bridging vein; the dura is attached to the skull so the bleed cannot cross the falx / tentorium.

Source of blood → nature of bleed

  • Meningeal artery → epidural haematoma.
  • Dural venous sinus → subdural or epidural haematoma.
  • Vein at its attachment to a sinus (bridging vein) → subdural haematoma.
  • Cerebral artery or vein → subarachnoid, intraparenchymal or intraventricular haemorrhage.

Brain herniations following injury

  • Supratentorial (above the tentorial notch): 1 central (transtentorial); 2 uncal (transtentorial) — can result in coma / death; 3 cingulate (subfalcine); 4 transcalvarial.
  • Infratentorial: 1 upward cerebellar (transtentorial); 2 downward cerebellar — tonsillar — rapidly fatal.

Chiari malformation

  • Cerebellar tonsils forced down into the foramen magnum / spinal canal because of abnormal development or lack of space in the cranial vault; several types of increasing severity that may affect the brainstem.
  • Type II (Arnold-Chiari): cerebellar vermal displacement with hydrocephalus and possible spina bifida.

The named brainstem syndromes are all the same trick: a cranial nerve on the side of the lesion plus a long tract deficit on the opposite side, so you localise by which nerve is caught. Weber is medial midbrain: oculomotor palsy on the same side, hemiplegia on the other. Benedikt is a little deeper into the tegmentum, so add the red nucleus: same third nerve palsy, but now the opposite limbs have tremor and ataxia instead of, or as well as, weakness. The basilar branches explain the pontine ones: paramedian branches supply the medial pons, where the corticospinal tract, the abducens and the facial nerve live, hence Millard-Gubler.

The venous side: superficial veins drain into the superior sagittal sinus and the cavernous sinus; the deep veins join into the great vein of Galen, which meets the inferior sagittal sinus to form the straight sinus. Everything reaches the transverse and sigmoid sinuses and leaves through the jugular foramen. And in the cavernous sinus, nerves three, four, V1 and V2 are in the wall, while six and the carotid run free inside, which is why the abducens is hit first.

Teaching script

The basal ganglia vocabulary trips people up, so fix it now. Striatum is caudate plus putamen. Lentiform is putamen plus globus pallidus. Corpus striatum is all three. Pallidum is just the globus pallidus.

The circuit is a brake and a release. Cortex excites the striatum. In the direct pathway the striatum inhibits the internal pallidum, which was inhibiting the thalamus, so the thalamus is released and the cortex goes: movement on. In the indirect pathway the striatum inhibits the external pallidum, which was inhibiting the subthalamic nucleus; the subthalamic nucleus, the only excitatory node inside the loop, then drives the internal pallidum, which clamps the thalamus: movement off. Dopamine from the substantia nigra pushes both the same way: it excites D1 direct neurons and inhibits D2 indirect neurons, so dopamine means go.

The functions are movement selection, initiation and suppression, tone and timing, procedural learning and some executive function. Blood supply: lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral for the putamen and capsule, the anterior choroidal for the medial pallidum, and the recurrent artery of Heubner from the anterior cerebral for the head of the caudate.

Terminology

  • Corpus striatum (dorsal striatum): caudate nucleus, putamen and globus pallidus.
  • Lentiform nucleus: putamen and globus pallidus.
  • Neostriatum (striatum): caudate nucleus and putamen.
  • Paleostriatum (pallidum): globus pallidus.
  • Horizontal and coronal sections: caudate head, putamen, globus pallidus externus / internus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra.

Basal ganglia pathways

  • Direct pathway — 'turning OFF the thing that causes inhibition': net effect turns on the motor cortex; disinhibition of thalamic control of motor planning.
  • Indirect pathway — 'turning ON the thing that causes inhibition': net effect turns off the motor cortex; activation of inhibition.
  • Circuit: motor cortex → striatum ⊣ GPi / SN (direct) or striatum ⊣ GPe ⊣ subthalamic nucleus → GPi (indirect) → thalamus (VL, VA) → motor cortex; substantia nigra → striatum via D2 (indirect) and D1 (direct).

Functions of basal ganglia

  • Movement regulation (planning and executing smooth, controlled voluntary movements; tone, timing, intensity); movement initiation and suppression (initiate desired, inhibit unwanted movements); coordination with motor cortex and cerebellum; procedural learning (riding a bicycle, instruments); executive functions (decision-making, planning, working memory).

Blood supply of basal ganglia

  • Lenticulostriate branches of the MCA; medial globus pallidus often by the anterior choroidal artery; head of the caudate by the recurrent artery of Heubner (ACA).

Teaching script

The pineal gland is a circumventricular organ: no blood brain barrier, rich blood supply, and it calcifies, which is why it shows on CT. It makes melatonin when it is dark, and the signal to make it takes a remarkable route: retina to suprachiasmatic nucleus, down to the intermediolateral column of the cord, out to the superior cervical ganglion, and back up along the carotid to the pineal. A pineal tumour presses on the dorsal midbrain and gives Parinaud syndrome: cannot look up, sluggish pupils, and hydrocephalus from a squashed aqueduct.

The thalamus is a relay table, and the specific relays are the ones tested. Lateral geniculate for vision to area seventeen. Medial geniculate for hearing to forty-one and forty-two. VPL takes the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract for the body; VPM takes the trigeminal and taste fibres for the face. VL and VA take cerebellar and pallidal input to motor cortex. The anterior nucleus takes the mammillothalamic tract to the cingulate gyrus, the memory loop. The non-specific nuclei, dorsomedial, pulvinar and intralaminar, feed association cortex, emotion and arousal.

Thalamic blood comes from the posterior communicating and posterior cerebral arteries; a posterolateral thalamic stroke gives Dejerine-Roussy, numbness that turns into burning contralateral pain.

The hypothalamus is grouped into preoptic, anterior, tuberal, posterior and lateral areas. The functional pairs to memorise: suprachiasmatic for circadian rhythm, supraoptic and paraventricular for ADH and oxytocin, anterior for cooling and posterior for heat conservation, ventromedial for satiety and lateral for hunger, arcuate for releasing hormones and dopamine, and the mammillary bodies for memory.

Pineal gland

  • Develops from neuroectoderm, lies caudally in the diencephalon; attached to the roof of the 3rd ventricle; visible on CT / MRI because 'brain sand' calcifies.
  • A circumventricular organ — lacks a blood–brain barrier and has rich vasculature, so the CNS interacts with peripheral blood for neuroendocrine functions; endocrine gland for seasonal and sleep–wake cycles; melatonin from pinealocytes is promoted by darkness and inhibited by light.
  • Parinaud syndrome: dorsal midbrain lesion at the superior colliculus, usually pineal tumour — weakness of upward gaze (sunset eyes), pupillary light-reflex abnormalities, non-communicating hydrocephalus.

Pineal gland: function ('biological clock')

  • Retina → suprachiasmatic nucleus (then paraventricular nucleus) → reticular formation in the ventrolateral medulla → intermediolateral (preganglionic) neurons of the cord → superior cervical ganglion → pineal gland — 'a circuitous loop'.

Thalamus — regions and nuclei

  • Anterior, medial and lateral regions; every nucleus relays to cortex. Anterior region: anterior nuclei; medial: dorsomedial; lateral: ventral subgroup (VA, VL, VPL, VPM) and dorsal subgroup (lateral dorsal, lateral posterior, pulvinar); metathalamic: MGN, LGN.

Thalamic nuclei table

  • Specific relay — LGN: optic tract → area 17 (vision); MGN: brachium of inferior colliculus → 41, 42 (hearing); VPL: medial lemniscus & spinothalamic → 3,1,2 (pain, temperature, touch, proprioception — body); VPM: trigeminothalamic + solitary nucleus → 3,1,2 (face + taste); VL: dentate, SN, GP → 4, 6 (movement); VA: GP, SN → 4, 6; anterior: mammillothalamic tract → cingulate (emotion, memory, limbic); lateral dorsal: visual cortex subdivisions → parietal (limbic).
  • Non-specific (association) — lateral posterior: parietal ↔ parietal; pulvinar: parietal / occipital / temporal → association cortex (vision recognition / attention?); dorsomedial: spinothalamic, amygdala, entorhinal → prefrontal (emotional response to pain, memory).
  • Non-specific (regulators) — intralaminar (centromedian): cortex, RF, cerebellum, BG, spinothalamic → frontal / parietal, caudate, putamen (alertness, pain response, BG and limbic modulation).

Thalamus — blood supply

  • 1 posterior communicating → anterior thalamoperforating; 2 posterior cerebral → posterior choroidal + posterior thalamoperforating; 3 anterior choroidal (LGB).

Thalamic pain syndrome (Dejerine-Roussy)

Teaching script

The cerebellum has three functional zones, and each has three names, an input, an output nucleus and a deficit. The vestibulocerebellum is the flocculonodular lobe, the oldest part, taking input from the vestibular system; it runs balance and eye movements through the fastigial nucleus, and its lesion gives truncal ataxia, nystagmus and vertigo. The spinocerebellum is the vermis and anterior lobe, taking the spinocerebellar tracts; it tunes ongoing movement and tone through the interposed nuclei, globose and emboliform, and its lesion gives limb ataxia mostly in the legs and slurred speech. The pontocerebellum is the big lateral hemispheres, the newest part, taking cortical input via the pons; it plans movement through the dentate nucleus, and its lesion gives dysmetria, intention tremor and hypotonia.

The deep nuclei from lateral to medial are dentate, emboliform, globose, fastigial: don't eat greasy foods. Pair them with the zones: dentate with the hemispheres, interposed with the vermis, fastigial with the flocculonodular lobe.

The cortex has three layers, molecular outside, then the Purkinje layer, then granule cells inside, and five cell types: stellate, basket, Purkinje, Golgi and granule. Purkinje cells are the only output, and they are inhibitory.

The circuit is simpler than the diagram. Two excitatory inputs arrive: mossy fibres from everything except the inferior olive, which end on granule cells, and climbing fibres from the inferior olive, which wrap the Purkinje cells directly. Both also send a copy straight to the deep nuclei. Granule cells send parallel fibres up to the Purkinje dendrites and to the inhibitory interneurons, basket, stellate and Golgi, which shape the signal. The Purkinje cell adds it all up and sends an inhibitory verdict to the deep nucleus, and the deep nucleus is what actually talks to the motor system.

Three tracts bring the spinal information in, and the exam point is where each one crosses. The dorsal spinocerebellar tract starts in Clarke's column, T1 to L2, stays on its own side, and enters through the inferior cerebellar peduncle; it carries the lower limb and trunk. The cuneocerebellar tract is the same thing for the upper limb, relayed in the accessory cuneate nucleus, also through the inferior peduncle. The ventral spinocerebellar tract is the odd one: it starts in the spinal border cells, crosses in the cord, climbs, and crosses back in the brainstem to enter through the superior cerebellar peduncle. Two crossings equal no crossing, so all three end up ipsilateral, and that is why cerebellar signs are on the side of the lesion.

  • Inhibitory cortical side loop: parallel fibres, basket cell, stellate cell, Golgi cell, granule cell, Purkinje cell. Teaching script

Think of the cerebellum as a comparator. It receives a copy of what the motor cortex intended, it receives sensory feedback about what actually happened, and it sends corrections back to the motor centres. The difference between intended and actual is what it fixes, and that is coordination.

Ataxia is the failure of that. Midline lesions of the vermis and flocculonodular lobe give truncal ataxia: wide stance, drunken gait, falling toward the side of the lesion on tandem walking. Hemisphere lesions give appendicular ataxia: dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor and hypotonia in the limbs.

Friedreich ataxia is the classic hereditary case: autosomal recessive, a GAA repeat in the frataxin gene on chromosome nine. It degenerates the spinocerebellar tracts, the dorsal columns, the corticospinal tracts and the dorsal root ganglia, so the ataxia is both sensory and cerebellar, and it comes with cardiomyopathy and diabetes.

General plan of cerebellar pathways

  • 1 corollary motor discharge: input from motor structures conveying the 'intended' results; 2 sensory feedback: exteroceptive and proprioceptive info about the 'results' on muscles and the external world; 3 modulatory output to motor centres: the cerebellum COMPARES intended vs actual and computes adjustments → smooth synergy = COORDINATION.
  • Diagrams of the neocerebellar (pontocerebellar), paleocerebellar and vestibulocerebellar systems.

Clinical correlations — ataxia

  • Ataxia = disordered contractions of agonist and antagonist muscles, lack of coordination between joints.
  • Truncal ataxia: lesions of vermis / flocculonodular lobe — loss of equilibrium, wide stance, veering to one side, unsteady 'drunk-like' gait; tandem gait test — patients fall toward the side of the lesion.
  • Appendicular ataxia: lesions of cerebellar hemispheres / lateral zone — lateral motor systems, skilled / fine planned movements; errors in force, direction, amplitude, speed; dysdiadochokinesia (can't pronate / supinate), hypotonia, dysmetria; can't trace figure A.

Friedreich's ataxia

  • Autosomal recessive, chromosome 9, GAA trinucleotide expansion in the frataxin gene → reduced frataxin (mitochondrial matrix protein, iron storage); half of all hereditary ataxias.
  • Degeneration of spinocerebellar tracts, dorsal columns, corticospinal tracts, dorsal root ganglia and peripheral nerves; dentate nuclei, Clarke's column and cerebellar peduncles may be affected; 'sensory ataxia' (dorsal columns) + 'motor ataxia' (cerebellar); muscle weakness, diabetes and heart disease. Teaching script

Smell breaks two rules. First, the receptor cells are themselves neurons: bipolar cells in the nasal mucosa whose thin axons pass through the cribriform plate and synapse in the glomeruli of the olfactory bulb on mitral and tufted cells. Second, those second-order axons run in the olfactory tract straight to the piriform cortex, the amygdala and the entorhinal cortex, without a thalamic relay. Smell reaches cortex first and the thalamus only afterwards, on its way to the orbitofrontal cortex. The lateral olfactory stria goes to the piriform cortex, the medial stria to the limbic forebrain, which is why smells are so tightly bound to memory and emotion. A cribriform fracture shears these axons and gives anosmia.

The optic nerve is also special: it is a tract of the central nervous system, wrapped in meninges, myelinated by oligodendrocytes, and made of retinal ganglion cell axons. Olfactory nerve CN I — pathway

  • 1st order = olfactory receptor cells: bipolar neurons; single dendrite ends in the olfactory mucosa as an expanded olfactory knob; single unmyelinated axon penetrates the cribriform plate (these axons do not form a single nerve).
  • 2nd order = mitral & tufted cells in the olfactory bulb; form the olfactory tract.
  • 3rd order = primary olfactory (piriform) cortex — the olfactory system does not relay through the thalamus.
  • Steps: 1 odorants bind receptors; 2 receptor cells fire; 3 relayed in glomeruli; 4 transmitted to higher regions. Targets: olfactory tubercle, piriform cortex, amygdala, entorhinal cortex.

Olfactory system pathway & coding — two tracts

  • Lateral olfactory stria: to primary olfactory (piriform) cortex → entorhinal, amygdala, hippocampus (perforant pathway); thalamus → prefrontal cortex.
  • Medial olfactory stria: bilaterally to limbic / forebrain structures; contralateral anterior olfactory nucleus, basal forebrain.

CN II — optic nerve

  • Figures: optic canals in the skull base, optic nerve in the orbit, chiasm → optic tracts → lateral geniculate bodies → optic radiations with overlapping visual fields.

Four exceptions to the corticobulbar rule, each a classic sign. Facial: the nucleus is split; the dorsal half serves the upper face and follows the rule, the ventral half serves the lower face and gets crossed fibres only. So a stroke above the nucleus weakens the opposite lower face with the forehead spared, while Bell palsy takes the whole same side. Accessory: ipsilateral only, so an upper motor neuron lesion weakens head turning to the opposite side and shoulder shrug on the same side, the same picture as a lower lesion. Hypoglossal: contralateral only, so an upper lesion makes the tongue deviate away from the lesion, a lower lesion makes it deviate toward it. Vagus is not an exception, but the uvula is small enough to show asymmetry anyway: it deviates toward an upper lesion and away from a lower one.

Then the descending tracts get sorted two ways. By position in the cord: lateral tracts, the lateral corticospinal and rubrospinal, drive the limbs; ventromedial tracts, the vestibulospinal, reticulospinal, tectospinal and anterior corticospinal, drive the trunk and posture. The corticospinal tract itself comes thirty percent from area four, thirty from area six and forty from the somatosensory cortex, runs through the posterior limb of the capsule, the crus, the pons and the pyramid, and crosses at the bottom of the medulla. Lesion signatures for the cortex: primary motor gives paralysis, supplementary motor gives akinesia and loss of bimanual coordination, premotor gives apraxia.

Exceptions — facial

  • The facial motor nucleus is split: the dorsal part innervates the upper quadrant of the face and follows the rule (bilateral, contralateral dominance); the ventral part innervates the lower quadrant and receives contralateral fibres only.
  • UMN lesion (corticobulbar fibres above the nucleus, e.g. stroke): weakness of the contralateral lower half of the face, forehead spared = supranuclear palsy. LMN lesion (nucleus or nerve after it exits): ipsilateral weakness of upper and lower face — cannot wrinkle the forehead or close the eye = Bell's palsy.

Exceptions — accessory

Exceptions — hypoglossal

The corticospinal tract in one sentence: cortex to caudal medulla, ninety percent cross there to become the lateral corticospinal tract for the limbs, ten percent stay uncrossed as the anterior corticospinal tract for the trunk and cross at their own segment. Everything else descending from the brainstem belongs to the medial motor system for posture, with one exception. Lateral vestibulospinal: from the lateral vestibular nucleus, uncrossed, to all levels but mainly lumbar, and it drives the extensors that keep you upright. Medial vestibulospinal: from the medial nucleus, bilateral, cervical only, for head position. Medial or pontine reticulospinal: uncrossed, facilitates, raises tone through the gamma motor neurons; think go. Lateral or medullary reticulospinal: bilateral, inhibits, lowers tone; think stop. Tectospinal: from the superior colliculus, crosses in the midbrain, cervical only, turns the head toward what you see.   • UMN from cortex → medulla (decussates at the caudal medulla) → lateral corticospinal → LMN; a second UMN stays uncrossed as the anterior corticospinal (balance), crossing near its termination in the ventral white commissure. Two annotated cord sections.

  • Origin red nucleus; decussation midbrain, contralateral; termination cervical cord lamina VI–VIII; facilitates motor neurons of proximal flexor musculature; functionally parallel to the lateral corticospinal tract; small in humans, may take over after corticospinal injury.

Four fibre classes, in order of size and speed. A-alpha, the fastest, is proprioception from spindles and tendon organs. A-beta is touch and vibration. A-delta is thin but myelinated, the sharp first pain and cold. C fibres are unmyelinated and slow, the dull burning second pain, warmth and itch. Rapidly adapting receptors fire at the start and end of a stimulus, slowly adapting ones keep firing and encode intensity.

When tissue is injured it releases an inflammatory soup, bradykinin, serotonin, prostaglandins, histamine, protons, ATP, that sensitises the nociceptors. The axon reflex is a related trick: an impulse travelling backwards down other branches of the same sensory fibre releases substance P onto vessels and mast cells, with no synapse involved, giving the red spot, the flare and the wheal.

In the dorsal horn, C fibres end in lamina two; lamina five receives everything, A-beta, A-delta, C and visceral afferents. That convergence is referred pain: heart and arm share the same lamina five neurons, so the brain blames the skin. And trigeminal neuralgia is the same nerve fibres firing from a compressing artery, treated with carbamazepine.

Primary afferent — 4 types of specialised axons

  • A-alpha: large myelinated, proprioception from skeletal muscle (Ia, Ib — spindles, GTOs); 13–20 µm, 80–120 m/s.
  • A-beta: medium myelinated, mechanoreception (touch, vibration, pressure); 6–12 µm, 35–75 m/s.
  • A-delta: small myelinated, pain and temperature; 1–5 µm, 5–30 m/s — fast, thinly myelinated, sharp intense 'first pain' ('Ow!').
  • C: small unmyelinated, pain, temperature, itch; 0.2–1.5 µm, 0.5–2 m/s — slower, throbbing, burning 'second pain' ('Aahh').

Rapidly vs slowly adapting receptors

  • Slowly adapting: respond throughout a stimulus; as pressure increases the total number of action potentials rises and firing rates are higher. Rapidly adapting: respond only at the start and end, signalling when applied and removed.

Upper versus lower motor neuron is the first question in any weakness case. Upper motor neurons live in the cortex and brainstem and their axons descend, mostly in the corticospinal tract; lower motor neurons live in the ventral horn and cranial motor nuclei and go out to the muscle. An upper lesion gives spastic weakness with increased tone, brisk reflexes, clonus and a Babinski sign, and little atrophy. A lower lesion gives flaccid weakness with reduced tone, absent reflexes, marked atrophy and fasciculations, and no Babinski.

Then the cord syndromes, which are just the tract map applied. Complete transection loses everything below the level, both sides. Brown-Séquard, a hemisection, loses motor and position sense on the same side, because those tracts cross in the brainstem, and pain and temperature on the opposite side, because the spinothalamic tract crossed at the segment. Syringomyelia is a cavity around the central canal, usually cervical, that first destroys the crossing spinothalamic fibres: a cape of lost pain and temperature over the shoulders and arms, with touch and vibration intact because the dorsal columns are out of reach. Subacute combined degeneration is B12 deficiency demyelinating the dorsal columns and the lateral corticospinal tracts: lost vibration and position sense with a positive Romberg, plus spastic weakness, and the pain fibres are spared.

Upper & lower motor neurons

A reflex is a response that happens without the brain: receptor, sensory neuron through the dorsal root ganglion, a synapse or two in the grey matter, motor neuron, muscle. The receptor for the stretch reflex is the muscle spindle, a bundle of small intrafusal fibres inside the muscle. Tap the tendon and the muscle contracts: here is the wiring. The Ia fibre from the stretched spindle enters the cord and synapses directly on the alpha motor neuron of the same muscle, which contracts to cancel the stretch. That single synapse makes the myotatic reflex the only monosynaptic reflex in the body. At the same time the Ia fibre excites an inhibitory interneuron onto the antagonist, so the antagonist relaxes: reciprocal inhibition.

The gamma motor neuron solves a problem. When a muscle shortens, its spindle would go slack and stop reporting. So whenever the upper motor neuron fires an alpha motor neuron it fires the gamma one too, alpha-gamma coactivation, and the gamma neuron tightens the intrafusal fibres so the spindle stays sensitive throughout the movement. The tested reflexes, biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, knee and ankle, are all this circuit at different root levels.

  • Sensory neuron (Ia fibre) from the muscle spindle → cord → α motor neuron → extrafusal fibre of the same muscle.

Reciprocal innervation

  • Minimises the antagonistic force and increases the effectiveness of the stretch reflex: the Ia signal also synapses with an inhibitory interneuron onto the antagonist's motor neuron.

  • An UMN activates α and γ neurons together; γ maintains tension in the intrafusal fibres (preserves sensitivity) so the spindle keeps sensing stretch while the muscle shortens.

Three more reflexes and the shock states. The Golgi tendon reflex is the opposite of the stretch reflex: the tendon organ, wired in series with the muscle, senses tension through a Ib fibre; the Ib fibre excites an inhibitory interneuron onto the same muscle's alpha motor neuron, so the muscle relaxes before it tears. It is polysynaptic.

The withdrawal reflex uses A-delta and C pain fibres, which through interneurons excite the flexors and inhibit the extensors of the same limb; you pull your hand off the stove before you know it is hot, and a drunk or unconscious person does not. The crossed-extensor reflex is its partner: the same input excites the extensors of the opposite limb so you do not fall over.

Root levels: biceps C5, brachioradialis C6, triceps C7, knee L3 and 4, ankle S1. Grading zero to four, with four meaning clonus, the sign of an upper motor neuron lesion.

Spinal shock is the temporary silence after a cord injury, every reflex and function below the level lost; it evolves through four phases from areflexia in the first day to late hyper-reflexia over months. Neurogenic shock is different: it is the haemodynamic collapse when the sympathetic outflow is cut, hypotension with bradycardia and a warm dry body.

  • If a muscle carries more load than it can handle the reflex prevents serious damage: relaxation and lengthening in response to contraction. Withdrawal (flexor) reflex

Crossed-extensor reflex

  • Complements the flexor reflex by maintaining balance when a limb is withdrawn (stepping on a sharp object); Aδ / C afferents synapse on interneurons of both sides: same side excite flexors (hamstrings), inhibit extensors (quadriceps); opposite side excite extensors (support body weight), inhibit flexors. The reticular formation is the loose network of nuclei running through the core of the brainstem, working in both directions, and it looks after the things every vertebrate needs: heart rate, breathing, reflexes and wakefulness.

For pain it adds the feeling. The reticulospinal system is the brainstem's own motor output, and it runs on a loop with the cerebellum and takes instructions from the premotor and motor cortex. Two nuclei matter: the nucleus reticularis pontis oralis in the pons, whose medial reticulospinal tract excites the anti-gravity extensors and facilitates spinal reflexes, and the nucleus gigantocellularis in the medulla, whose lateral reticulospinal tract inhibits them. Together they set tone and posture.

The same fibres carry autonomic commands. Cardiovascular control lives in the medulla: baroreceptor and chemoreceptor input arrives through nine and ten into the solitary nucleus; the output is the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus slowing the heart and the reticulospinal fibres driving the sympathetic chain to speed it up. This is why tonsillar herniation kills: the cerebellar tonsils push through the foramen magnum, compress the medulla, and breathing and circulation stop.

Lateral RST: inhibition of axial extensors and of the MRT; medullary (lateral) tract inhibits (−) spinal reflexes. Together: modulation of muscle tone, regulation of posture (α and γ motor neurons).

Autonomic — cardiovascular

Afferents to the medullary cardiovascular centres: chemoreceptors and baroreceptors — GVA from CN IX and X via the solitary pathway; hypothalamus and higher cortex. From the lateral geniculate the optic radiations fan out, and the fan splits into two streams by quadrant. The geniculate itself has six layers: the contralateral eye feeds layers one, four and six, the ipsilateral eye layers two, three and five. Field defects follow from one anatomical fact: the nasal retina of each eye sees the temporal field and crosses at the chiasm; the temporal retina sees the nasal field and stays on its own side. So a lesion of the optic nerve blinds one eye. A lesion of the middle of the chiasm, classically a pituitary adenoma, craniopharyngioma or meningioma, cuts both sets of crossing nasal fibres and takes away both temporal fields: bitemporal hemianopia. A right tract lesion, carrying the left nasal retina and the right temporal retina, loses the left field of both eyes.

Visual-field mapping

  • Binocular field with left and right visual fields (A–D), fixation point; left temporal field falls on the right nasal retina / left nasal field on the left temporal retina — temporal retina sees the nasal field; nasal retinal fibres cross at the chiasm into the contralateral optic tract.

Optic nerve

  • Axons ultimately project to the LGN of the thalamus (the 'other' axons follow the other 3 pathways); a complete lesion of the optic nerve = complete monocular visual loss (blind eye).

Optic chiasm: bitemporal hemianopia

Optic chiasm: lateral aspect

Internuclear ophthalmoplegia is a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus. Teaching script

The auditory pathway, and the one fact that matters most is in the middle of it. Spiral ganglion, cochlear nerve, then the cochlear nuclei: the ventral nucleus projects to both sides, the dorsal to the opposite side, and these nuclei are the only station that hears from one ear alone. From there the trapezoid body crosses, the superior olive compares the two ears, the lateral lemniscus climbs to the inferior colliculus, the medial geniculate relays to Heschl's gyrus. Because everything past the cochlear nuclei is bilateral, a central lesion never causes deafness in one ear; one-sided deafness means cochlea, nerve or nuclei.

Two kinds of deafness. Conductive: the sound cannot reach the cochlea, from wax, otitis media or otosclerosis fixing the stapes. Sensorineural: cochlea or nerve, from presbycusis at the base of the cochlea taking the high frequencies, from ototoxic drugs like aminoglycosides and aspirin, from congenital rubella or CMV, or from an acoustic neuroma. Rinne and Weber sort them: in conductive loss bone beats air and Weber goes to the bad ear; in sensorineural loss air still beats bone and Weber goes to the good ear.

The brainstem evoked response reads the pathway as seven waves, nerve, nerve, cochlear nucleus, superior olive, lateral lemniscus, inferior colliculus, medial geniculate; and otoacoustic emissions, sounds the outer hair cells make when clicked, are the newborn screen, absent once the loss is thirty decibels or more.

Auditory pathway order (simplified)

  • Spiral ganglion → auditory nerve → ventral (project bilaterally) and dorsal (project contralaterally) cochlear nuclei — the only auditory nuclei that do not receive binaural input → trapezoid body (decussating ventral-nucleus fibres) → superior olive → lateral lemniscus → inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body → auditory cortex (transverse gyrus of Heschl, superior temporal gyrus). Figure: auditory radiations in the sublenticular internal capsule, brachium and commissure of the inferior colliculus, nucleus and commissure of the lateral lemniscus, inner / outer hair cells, tectorial and basilar membranes.

Conductive vs sensorineural

  • Conductive: sound waves can't convert to nerve signals — obstruction (wax), infection (otitis media), otosclerosis (bony overgrowth of stapes). Sensorineural: cochlear or nerve disease — cochlear damage, cochlear-nerve failure (acoustic neuroma), cranial-nerve damage.

1 Conduction deafness

  • Interrupted passage through the external / middle ear, often reversible: a wax / foreign body in the meatus; b otosclerosis — neogenesis of labyrinthine spongy bone around the oval window fixing the stapes (most frequent cause of progressive conduction deafness); c otitis media — most common cause of meningitis (excluding meningococcus) and of brain abscesses.

2 Nerve deafness (sensorineural)

  • Disease of the cochlea, cochlear nerve or central connections (acoustic neuroma); drugs and toxins (quinine, aspirin, streptomycin); prolonged loud noise; rubella in utero, CMV, syphilis; usually permanent: a presbycusis — degeneration of the organ of Corti at the base of the cochlea, high-frequency loss 4000–8000 Hz, most common cause of hearing loss; b acoustic neuroma (schwannoma / neurilemoma) of CN VIII in the internal auditory meatus or cerebellopontine angle — unilateral deafness and tinnitus.

Rinne test

  • Normal air > bone; conductive loss bone > air (cannot hear next to the ear); sensorineural loss air > bone (both reduced, air remains > bone); unilateral loss: Rinne A > B → sensorineural, B > A → conductive.

Weber test

  • Normal or bilateral loss: signal equal both ears (midline); conductive: louder in the bad ear (no background noise); sensorineural: louder in the good ear (no nerve to sense vibration).

Brainstem evoked response audiometry (BERA / BAEP)

  • Objective non-invasive test of electrical activity from the inner ear to the inferior colliculus within the first 10 ms; seven waves (I, III, V most visible): I distal auditory nerve; II proximal nerve; III cochlear nucleus; IV superior olivary complex; V lateral lemniscus; VI inferior colliculus; VII medial geniculate body.

Otoacoustic emissions (OAEs)

  • Simple, rapid, cost-effective neonatal screening in the first days; a pre-neural objective indicator of healthy cochlear function; obstruction of outer / middle / inner ear → no OAE. TEOAE — transient evoked, generated by the outer hair cells in response to a click (cochlear amplifier 750 Hz – 4 kHz); undetectable if sensory loss ≥ 30 dB HL or middle-ear abnormality. DPOAE — distortion product (non-linear); SOAE — spontaneous pure tones in 60–70 % of normal ears.

The vestibular system shares the eighth nerve and the otic-vesicle origin with hearing, but it has two jobs: keeping you balanced, and keeping your eyes on a target while your head moves. There are five sense organs in the membranous labyrinth. Three cristae, one in the ampulla of each semicircular canal, anterior, horizontal and posterior, sense rotation in three planes through hair cells embedded in the gelatinous cupula. Two maculae, in the utricle and the saccule, carry otoconia on their otolithic membrane and sense linear acceleration and gravity. The cell bodies of the vestibular nerve sit in Scarpa's ganglion in the internal auditory meatus.

The hair cells are the same mechanotransducers as in the cochlea, but they keep one true cilium, the kinocilium, on one edge of the bundle. Bend the bundle toward the kinocilium and the cell depolarises; away and it hyperpolarises. Type I cells sit in a calyx ending near the striola of the maculae; type II cells have ordinary boutons.

Vestibular system

  • Served by CN VIII (SSA); derived from the otic vesicle (otic placode, surface ectoderm); two functions: 1 posture, balance, equilibrium; 2 coordinating head and eye movements for visual fixation while the head moves.

Vestibular organs

The utricle lies horizontal and the saccule vertical, so the utricle reports forward and sideways acceleration and the saccule reports gravity and vertical movement. The three canals each open into the utricle, and the ampulla at one end holds the crista and cupula that bend with angular acceleration; anything that is not straight up and down or left and right is theirs. The horizontal canals tilt about thirty degrees back, and the anterior canal on one side lies in the same plane as the posterior canal on the other, so the pairs work as push-pull partners.

The vestibular nuclei send their output three ways: up the medial longitudinal fasciculus to the eye-movement nuclei for the vestibulo-ocular reflex, down the lateral vestibulospinal and the descending MLF to the ventral horn for posture, and through the juxtarestiform body to the cerebellum. The reflex has two circuits: for vertical eye movement the canals drive the superior vestibular nucleus, which reaches the trochlear and oculomotor nuclei through the MLF; for horizontal movement they drive the medial vestibular nucleus, which reaches the abducens and then, through the MLF, the oculomotor nucleus. The lateral vestibulospinal tract keeps the extensors on; the medial one adjusts the neck.

Utricle & saccule

  • Otoconia on the otolithic membrane over the macula (striola, kinocilium k, stereocilia s); utricle oriented horizontally, saccule vertically → utricle detects linear acceleration in the horizontal plane, saccule in the vertical plane (gravity sensor).

Semicircular canal ampullary organs

  • 3 canals — half-circle tubes of endolymph, both ends opening into the utricle; one end enlarged = ampulla containing hair cells in the ridge-like crista ampullaris under the cupula; angular acceleration displaces the cupula; canals sense the rotational position of the head.

Canal orientation

  • Horizontal canals tilted ~30°; left / right anterior canals and posterior canals at 45° to the sagittal plane so each anterior canal pairs with the contralateral posterior canal.

Vestibular nuclei projections

  • Through the ascending MLF to the ocular motor nuclei (III, IV, VI) → vestibulo-ocular reflexes; through the descending MLF and lateral vestibulospinal (Deiters') tract to ventral-horn motor neurons → postural reflexes; also vestibulothalamic tracts → ventral posterior nucleus → vestibular cortex; juxtarestiform body to the nodulus of the cerebellum.

Teaching script

Caloric testing pours cold or warm water into the ear of an unconscious patient and reads the nystagmus: COWS, cold makes the fast phase beat to the opposite side, warm to the same side. The doll's eye reflex is the bedside version: turn the head and, if the brainstem is intact, the eyes stay fixed forward by moving the opposite way; if they move with the head the brainstem is failing. The reflex is suppressed in a conscious person, so it only means something in coma.

Nystagmus without a moving visual scene means brainstem or cerebellum. Jerk nystagmus is a slow drift then a fast corrective saccade, and it is named for the fast phase. Vertigo is the illusion of spinning, with nausea, vomiting, tinnitus and unsteadiness. Peripheral vertigo, from the labyrinth or nerve, is severe, brief, positional and, the key discriminator, stops when the patient fixes their gaze; central vertigo from the brainstem is milder, chronic, and does not.

Two peripheral causes. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo is loose otoconia from the utricle drifting into a canal and weighing down the cupula, giving seconds of spinning on turning in bed, fixed by the Epley manoeuvre. A vestibular schwannoma is a Schwann-cell tumour of the eighth nerve at the cerebellopontine angle: one-sided hearing loss and tinnitus first, then facial numbness as it presses the trigeminal, then facial weakness from the facial nerve.

Caloric testing of patients in a coma

  • Cold and warm water irrigation (COWS — cold opposite, warm same for the fast phase in a conscious patient); panel: conscious normal vs unconscious with brainstem intact, MLF lesion (bilateral), low brainstem lesion.

Doll's eye reflex (oculocephalic)

  • Hold the eyelids open and briskly rotate the head side to side. Positive: eyes move opposite to the rotation and stay looking forward = intact brainstem. Negative: eyes move with the head = severe brainstem dysfunction; an oversimplification — some nystagmus can occur pathologically. Suppressed in a conscious adult with normal neurology; active in a comatose patient with gross brainstem function; absent if the reflex arc is damaged.

Nystagmus

  • Without any motion in the visual field = lesion of the brainstem or cerebellum. Pendular: fast-phase saccades in both directions. Jerk: slow drift one way, fast saccade the other; named for the saccade direction ('leftward-beating').

Vertigo

  • Illusion of spinning without actual movement, with nausea, vomiting, tinnitus, nystagmus, gait ataxia; debilitating. Peripheral (membranous labyrinth / semicircular ducts): more severe, intermittent, short, positional, more common (80 %), stops with visual fixation. Central (brainstem vestibular nuclei / pathways): mild, chronic, non-positional, does not stop with fixation.

Vestibular schwannoma

  • Acoustic neuroma / neurinoma / neurilemoma — benign slow-growing tumour of the vestibulocochlear nerve from over-production of Schwann cells (the 'onion-skin' wrap of nerve fibres); unilateral / asymmetric hearing loss, tinnitus, dizziness; can compress the trigeminal (facial numbness) and facial nerve (weakness / paralysis on the side of the tumour); large tumours press brainstem and cerebellum — life-threatening.

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)

  • Brief episodes of mild-to-intense dizziness, usually > 50 years, triggered by head-position changes (tipping, lying down, turning in bed); rarely serious except for falls; otoconia dislodged from the utricle / saccule float into a semicircular canal, stick in the cupula, add weight → abnormal canal stimulation; treated by the Epley manoeuvre driving the otolith back into the utricle / saccule.

Teaching script

Three odds and ends. Multiple system atrophy, once called Shy-Drager, combines parkinsonism, autonomic failure, cerebellar ataxia and pyramidal signs in varying mixtures. Complex regional pain syndrome follows an injury: type one, reflex sympathetic dystrophy, has no nerve damage, type two, causalgia, does; both give burning pain, exquisite sensitivity, swelling and colour and temperature change in a hand or foot, then atrophy of skin and bone, and treatment is hard. Familial dysautonomia, Riley-Day, is recessive on chromosome nine in Eastern European Jewish families: from birth the baby is floppy with absent tendon reflexes, does not feel pain or temperature properly, makes no tears and cannot control temperature or blood pressure. That floppy newborn has a differential running from kernicterus and cerebral palsy through spinal muscular atrophy and neuropathies to neonatal myasthenia and infant botulism.

Multiple system atrophy (clinical reasoning)

  • Formerly Shy-Drager: parkinsonism (bradykinesia + rigidity and / or tremor), autonomic failure, cerebellar ataxia, pyramidal signs in varying combinations.

Complex regional pain syndrome

  • After injury; Type I reflex sympathetic dystrophy (RSD, Sudeck's atrophy) — no apparent nerve damage; Type II causalgia — nerve damage present. Localised intense burning pain, sensitivity to the slightest vibration or touch, oedema, skin colour and temperature changes; hand or foot, often distant from a healed wound; atrophy of skin and subcutaneous tissue then bone. Pathogenesis unclear (NMDA?): hypothesis of electrical 'crosstalk' (ephapse) → sympathetic hyperactivity; management complex and often ineffective. Photos A / B of the feet.

Familial dysautonomia (Riley-Day)

  • Rare recessive mutation on chromosome 9, Eastern European Jewish ancestry; from birth: hypotonia, absent / decreased deep tendon reflexes, impaired pain and temperature sensation, lack of tears, defective temperature control, intestinal dysfunction, blood-pressure lability; always check hypotonia in the newborn; 50 % reach age 30.

Differential of floppy infant syndrome

  • CNS: kernicterus, perinatal asphyxia, cerebral palsy (atonic), chromosomal anomalies incl. Down's. Spinal cord: anterior horn disease, spinal muscular atrophy. Peripheral nerves: acute polyneuropathy, familial dysautonomia, congenital sensory neuropathy. Myoneural junction: neonatal myasthenia gravis, infantile botulism, after antibiotics (botulism symptoms acute vs the others).

Teaching script

Sleep in one table. Awake with eyes open is beta, fast and small; eyes closed is alpha. Stage one is light sleep, theta. Stage two is deeper, with sleep spindles and K complexes, and it is when teeth grinding happens; it is nearly half the night. Stage three is slow-wave delta sleep, the deepest, where sleepwalking, night terrors and bedwetting occur. REM is a quarter of the night: muscle tone gone, brain oxygen use up, pulse and pressure variable, acetylcholine up, dreams, and the eye movements are driven by the PPRF. It comes every ninety minutes and lengthens toward morning. The mnemonic is: at night, BATS drink blood. Depression shortens the time to the first REM period, so does narcolepsy, and the elderly lose both REM and slow-wave sleep.

Sleep stages (% of total sleep time, young adults)

  • Awake (eyes open): alert, active concentration — beta (highest frequency, lowest amplitude). Awake (eyes closed): alpha. Non-REM: N1 (5 %) light sleep — theta; N2 (45 %) deeper, bruxism ('twoth' grinding) — sleep spindles and K complexes; N3 (25 %) deepest / slow-wave, sleepwalking, night terrors, bedwetting ('wee and flee in N3') — delta (lowest frequency, highest amplitude). REM (25 %): loss of motor tone, ↑ brain O₂ use, variable pulse / BP, ↑ ACh; dreaming, nightmares, penile / clitoral tumescence; memory processing; extraocular movements from the PPRF; every 90 min, duration ↑ through the night — beta.
  • Elderly: ↓ REM, ↓ N3. Depression: ↑ REM time, ↓ REM latency, ↓ N3, repeated awakenings, early-morning awakening (terminal insomnia). Narcolepsy: ↓ REM latency. 'At night, BATS Drink Blood' (beta-alpha-theta-spindles-delta-beta).
